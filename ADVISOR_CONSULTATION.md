## Verbatim response

Hello, thank you for your question. First of all, I would like to emphasize that my studies in China focused on acupuncture, massage, and tuina, with an inclination towards physical therapy and rehabilitation.

Of course, we must also learn about the diagnosis of other diseases and the use of traditional Chinese medicine during our university years.

1. Pre-treatment medication inquiry and safe combination assessment: Before treating or recommending traditional Chinese medicine, it is essential to rigorously and proactively inquire about all Western medicines, proprietary Chinese medicines, and health supplements the patient is currently taking. Blindly combining Western and traditional Chinese medicines can lead to serious interactions, ranging from reduced efficacy to life-threatening conditions such as poisoning and bleeding.

To determine whether traditional Chinese medicine and Western medicine can be safely used together, clinical practice mainly uses a comprehensive assessment based on the following four dimensions:

1. Evaluation of the reproducibility of components：Screening compound traditional Chinese medicines: Many traditional Chinese medicines contain Western medicine ingredients (such as Ganmaoqing capsules and Xiaoke pills). It is essential to check the ingredient list to prevent the accumulation of similar ingredients with the Western medicines the patient is currently taking, which could lead to excessive dosage (such as acute liver damage caused by acetaminophen overdose).

2. Conflicts and Synergies in Assessing Pharmacological Effects：Anticoagulation/Blood-Activating Risks: Patients taking anticoagulants (such as warfarin and aspirin) may experience a significantly increased risk of bleeding if they combine these medications with traditional Chinese medicines that promote blood circulation and remove blood stasis, such as danshen, angelica, and safflower. Hypoglycemic/Hypertensive Synergistic Effects: Combining traditional Chinese medicine for lowering blood sugar with Western hypoglycemic drugs may trigger severe hypoglycemia.

3. Assess metabolic interferences in vivo (pharmacokinetics)：Affecting gastrointestinal absorption: Traditional Chinese medicines containing large amounts of tannins (such as Gallnut and Sanguisorba officinalis) should not be taken with iron supplements, enzyme preparations, or antibiotics, as tannins can combine with these Western medicines to form precipitates, hindering absorption. Liver enzyme induction and inhibition: Some traditional Chinese medicines (such as St. John's wort) can activate hepatic cytotoxic P450 enzymes, thereby accelerating the metabolism of immunosuppressants or antidepressants, leading to a sharp drop in the blood concentration of Western medicines and rendering them ineffective.

4. Assessing physicochemical properties, precipitation, and toxic reactions：Acid-base neutralization reaction: Acidic traditional Chinese medicines such as hawthorn and dried plum should not be taken with alkaline Western medicines such as sodium bicarbonate, otherwise the efficacy will be reduced due to acid-base neutralization. Increased alkaloid toxicity: Traditional Chinese medicines containing alkaloids such as aconite, prepared aconite root, and fritillaria should not be taken with Western medicines such as atropine and caffeine, as this will increase cardiotoxicity and neurotoxicity.

In clinical practice or routine recommendations, when dealing with complex combination therapies where interactions cannot be definitively identified, the principles of "safety first, postpone combination therapy, and seek verification from multiple sources" should be followed, and the following standard procedure should be adopted:

1. Consult professional tools and literature: Immediately search authoritative databases on drug interactions between Chinese and Western medicines. Alternatively, consult the Pharmacopoeia of the People's Republic of China and the latest clinical drug use guidelines to check the "contraindications" and "drug interactions" sections for relevant drugs.

2. Initiate multidisciplinary pharmaceutical consultation: Consult with clinical pharmacists in the hospital (especially traditional Chinese medicine pharmacists or pharmacists specializing in integrated traditional Chinese and Western medicine) or doctors in related specialties, so that professional pharmaceutical personnel can conduct a scientific evaluation of the chemical structure and pharmacological pathway.

3. Strict time separation should be implemented: If both medications must be taken before a clear conclusion is reached, patients must be instructed to take the traditional Chinese medicine and Western medicine at least 1 to 2 hours apart. This can completely avoid direct physical or chemical contact between the drugs in the gastrointestinal tract. However, it should be noted that this cannot eliminate pharmacological antagonism after absorption into the bloodstream.

4. Closely monitor and establish a follow-up mechanism: Inform patients that they must closely monitor their physical reactions during the initial stage of combined medication. Targeted and regular monitoring of key physiological indicators (such as blood pressure, blood sugar, INR value for coagulation function, liver and kidney function, etc.) is necessary. If dizziness, rash, bleeding tendency, or worsening of existing symptoms occur, medication must be stopped immediately and medical attention sought.

I cannot give a precise answer to your last question because I am not a professional in Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) pharmacology and therefore cannot provide one. However, I must emphasize that according to the latest Chinese Pharmacopoeia, there are currently 12,408 kinds of Chinese medicinal herbs. TCM practitioners typically use multiple herbs in combination according to different prescriptions. The dosage and types of herbs involved have too many uncertainties and variables. Trying to record pharmacological effects through a simple program would be an enormous undertaking and would require a high level of expertise.
